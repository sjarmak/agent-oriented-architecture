# The factory is an observatory

*Blog post draft to accompany `or-for-software-factories.md`. The opening scene is composite color; every citable observatory fact below is published, and bibcodes in brackets resolve at ADS.*

---

At 20:47 on Palomar Mountain, the marine layer starts climbing the ridge, and a computer in the dome of the 48-inch Samuel Oschin Telescope throws away its plan for the night. Nobody is alarmed. The Zwicky Transient Facility loses pieces of its plan most nights, to cloud, to seeing, to a target-of-opportunity alert from a gravitational-wave detector, and the scheduler's whole design assumes it: chop the night into blocks of thirty minutes, assign work to blocks by solving an integer program that maximizes how much sky gets searched per unit time, then, inside each block, solve a traveling-salesman tour so the telescope wastes as little time as possible slewing between targets [2019PASP..131f8003B]. When the clouds come, it re-solves. It has been doing this in production since 2018.

I have come to think of that dome as the most honest picture available of what an automated software factory is trying to be, and the longer you hold the two side by side, the less metaphorical the comparison feels. An observatory is a factory whose product is photons. It has a backlog: thousands of approved observation requests, each with a scientific priority, each waiting for its moment. It has agents: telescopes and instruments, some interchangeable, some specialized, each with a cost to start up and a cost to switch tasks. It has a hard budget that renews daily and cannot be banked: dark time, the hours between dusk and dawn, which is to an observatory what a token budget is to a fleet of coding agents. It has a review committee upstream deciding what deserves time, an unreliable environment that fails jobs through no fault of the work, and, in many facilities, one shared serial resource everybody's output must eventually pass through. Astronomy has been running this factory for decades, at scales from a single dome to a global network, and it has published what it learned.

## Two monasteries, one heresy each

The first thing the observatory record settles is a question every factory builder eventually loses sleep over: how much planning is the right amount of planning?

Two great survey telescopes answered it in opposite ways. ZTF is the planning monastery: a deterministic optimization solved fresh whenever conditions change, the whole night laid out block by block, provably good against its objective. The Vera Rubin Observatory, whose survey is bigger and whose nights are more valuable, committed the opposite heresy. Its scheduler holds no plan at all. Naghib and colleagues formulated the problem as a Markov decision process and then declined to solve it as one, replacing all of that machinery with a scoring function: a weighted sum of handcrafted features of the current moment, slew cost from here, sky brightness there, how long since this field was last visited, evaluated over every candidate, best score wins, decide again after the next exposure [2019AJ....157..151N]. The weights get tuned offline against a simulator. When weather destroys twenty minutes, nothing needs repair, because there was never a plan to break; the policy looks at the sky as it is and picks the next right thing.

Here is what should stop a factory architect mid-diagram: both monasteries beat the thing everyone assumes comes next. Explicit uncertainty modeling, scenario trees, probabilistic forecasts of which parts of the night will be lost, appears in this literature only as a preprint from 2025 [2025arXiv250403666R], after a decade in which the deployed systems handled the most quantifiable uncertainty imaginable, weather, by simply re-deciding cheaply and often. The factory translation writes itself. Your agents fail stochastically, your queue mutates hourly, and the temptation is to model all of it. The observatories suggest you should instead make re-decision nearly free, run a real optimization at coarse intervals the way ZTF does, score greedily but intelligently in between the way Rubin does, and let the weather be the weather.

## The sky does not wait for proofs

The second lesson is about perfectionism. When two neutron stars collide and LIGO issues an alert, ZTF's follow-up scheduler has to choose which fields to image, packed into valid observing blocks, to maximize the probability of catching the kilonova before it fades. This is a hard combinatorial problem, and the team runs it with a 500-second time limit on the solver. Across 951 simulated alerts, most instances reached proven optimality inside the limit; the rest shipped whatever the best-found schedule was when the clock ran out, and the authors note, almost as an aside, that a 100-second limit would have cost them only 64 truncated solves [2022ApJ...935...87P].

Every production scheduler in this literature works that way. Hard time cap, take the incumbent, treat the certificate of optimality as a luxury item. The reason is not sloppiness but a clear-eyed accounting of what optimality is worth when the problem will be different in an hour: a plan that is 3% off the true optimum but exists now beats a perfect plan for a night that has already changed. Factories inherit this directly. The epoch planner gets a deadline measured in seconds, it ships the best schedule found, and nobody waits on a proof, because the merge queue, like the sky, keeps moving.

There is a sharp edge to this pragmatism, though, and it cuts the other way. When Handley and colleagues compared their exact solver against a local-search heuristic on 360 telescope-sequencing instances, the heuristic reported "no feasible schedule" seven times on instances where the exact method proved a feasible schedule existed [2024AJ....167...33H]. The heuristic did not fail loudly; it lied quietly, and only the presence of an exact method revealed it. Any factory whose dispatcher can silently drop work, and most can, should hear that number as a warning: heuristics shrug, solvers certify, and you want at least one component in the system capable of saying *there was work to do and we provably missed it*.

## Fairness is a constraint with a coefficient

NASA's Deep Space Network schedules a small number of giant antennas against the competing demands of every active deep-space mission, including a spacecraft launched in 1977 that requires multiple antennas arrayed together just to hear it. The scheduling model that manages this does something factories mostly leave to hope: it writes fairness down as mathematics. Alongside the visibility windows and the setup and teardown time bracketing every track, the objective includes explicit terms balancing each mission's fraction of satisfied requests and the load across antennas, and the papers report the resulting fairness numbers the way they report throughput [2021arXiv211111628C].

Anyone who has watched an agent fleet knows the failure this prevents. One urgent epic arrives, and for three days every worker in the pool is feeding it while maintenance work rots and a smaller project's beads age past relevance. In most orchestrators the defense is a human noticing. In the DSN's formulation the defense is a coefficient, and starving Voyager is not something the schedule can do while claiming to be optimal. The same model carries the rest of the factory's session economics almost line for line: setup and teardown are the agent's clone-and-context-load tax, minimum track durations exist because a session too short to do useful work still pays full spin-up cost, and arraying, several antennas on one oversized request, is several agents co-assigned to one epic under a single completion constraint.

## Every dome built its own scheduler, and paid for it

The least flattering pattern in this literature is also the most familiar. Las Cumbres built a production ILP scheduler in 2014 [2015arXiv150307170L]. ZTF built one in 2018. WFST built one. IPROS built one for a longitude-distributed relay network in 2025 [2025RAA....25a5008J]. These are recognizably the same slot-assignment integer program, rebuilt from scratch by group after group who share conferences, journals, and in some cases hallways, and the first deliberately multi-mission, open-source scheduling framework, M4OPT, arrived only in 2025 [2025arXiv250217560S]. A field that shares a problem but not a kernel pays for the kernel over and over.

The software-factory ecosystem is speed-running the same pattern with dispatchers, every orchestration project hand-rolling its own priority heuristics, its own retry logic, its own capacity math, none of it shared, most of it unexamined. The observatories' belated correction suggests the fix is unglamorous: the scheduler is a product, not a per-team script, and the right time to make it shared and declarative is before the fifth reimplementation rather than after.

## Replay your own nights

The observatory analogy ends at a door the astronomers cannot walk through, because a factory has one advantage no dome will ever have: it can rerun the night. A telescope gets each sky exactly once, which is why survey teams lean so hard on simulators. A software factory records every arrival, every claim, every outcome, and can replay an identical recorded week under two different dispatch policies, holding the arrival noise fixed so that whatever differs is the policy. The datacenter-scheduling world already treats this as table stakes [2018arXiv181001963M]; among agent-orchestration systems it is nearly unheard of.

So the closing move belongs to the factory, and it is concrete. Take your recorded traces. Replay them under the dispatcher you have, then under a thirty-line priority index of the kind Rubin points its billion-dollar survey with, then, if the index earns it, under a small integer program of the kind that has run ZTF's nights for eight years. The observatories spent thirty-five years and a great deal of dark time learning which of these wins where. The replay costs you an afternoon, and unlike the sky, your nights will hold still while you measure them.
